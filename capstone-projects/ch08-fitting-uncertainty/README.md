# Capstone: Full AI-collaborative fitting project

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Chapter | 8 — Fitting Models to Data |
| Effort | about 15–25 hours over 3–4 weeks |
| Tier | chapter project — the student demonstrates that a fitting and uncertainty workflow holds up, and may do so on synthetic data |
| Deliverable | a fitted $SIR_I$-type model with uncertainty quantified and identifiability addressed |

Classified as a chapter project because the book permits synthetic data: what is being demonstrated
is that the workflow is sound, not that a conclusion about a particular epidemic is right. A student
who chooses real data and defends an epidemiological conclusion has effectively written a term
project, and can be graded as one.

## Prerequisites

Chapter 8 worked through, in particular §8.2 (Preprocessing Surveillance Data), §8.3 (What Can Be
Fit: An Identifiability Discussion), §8.4 (Least-Squares Fitting of $\alpha$) and §8.5 (Initial Conditions and
the Susceptible-Fraction Question). §8.7, the seasonal influenza case study, is the worked example
this project generalizes. Chapter 7's implementation is assumed — fitting a simulator you cannot
verify produces confident nonsense.

Substantial programming, including optimisation.

## Learning objectives

By the end, the student can:

1. Preprocess surveillance data with each choice recorded and its effect on the fit measured.
2. State which parameters are identifiable from the available data before attempting to fit them.
3. Fit by least squares with a defensible loss function and stated initial conditions.
4. Quantify uncertainty in fitted parameters, and distinguish that uncertainty from model error.
5. Recognize when a good fit is evidence of overparameterisation rather than of a correct model.

## The task

The book's specification, from Chapter 8's exercises:

> Fitting an $SIR_I$-type model to a real or synthetic outbreak dataset (suggested options include
> the chapter's seasonal influenza data, COVID-19 weekly incidence, or a synthetic dataset with
> realistic noise overlay), with full uncertainty quantification and AI-collaborative validation.

Synthetic data is not the soft option. It is the only route on which the true parameters are known,
so it is the only route where the uncertainty quantification can itself be checked. Students taking
the synthetic route are expected to report whether their intervals covered the truth.

## Materials

**Provided in this repository.** `notebooks/ch08/ch8_preprocessing.py` implements the chapter's
preprocessing levels and is the direct starting point for step 1.
`notebooks/ch08/ch8_influenza_fit.py` is the worked fit; `ch8_central_comparison.py` compares fitting
choices. The five probes in `notebooks/ch08/probes/` — `ch8_route_b_probe.py`,
`ch8_route_b_identifiability.py`, `ch8_route_b_ascertainment.py`, `ch8_route_b_lossfn_diagnostic.py`
and `ch8_route_b_robustness.py` — are diagnostic tools for exactly the questions step 2 asks, and
are the most under-used material in the repository. `src/sir_i_model.py` supplies the forward model.
`notebooks/ch09/ch9_figures.py` shows bootstrap and profile-likelihood machinery if the student goes
beyond the chapter.

**Student-supplied.** The dataset. **No epidemiological data is redistributed in this repository.**
The chapter's WSU 2009 H1N1 data is freely available from the MIDAS Network catalog at
<https://catalog.midasnetwork.us>, and `data/README.md` records its provenance and terms; download
it from the original source. COVID-19 weekly incidence is available from national public-health
agencies or the archived JHU CSSE repository. Synthetic data is generated by the student from a
known parameter set, with the generating script submitted.

**Deliberately not provided.** A fitted parameter set for any suggested dataset, and a recommended
loss function. Choosing the loss and defending it is graded.

## The six steps

1. **Parameter elicitation.** Before fitting, state which parameters will be fitted, which fixed,
   and on what basis. Fixing a parameter without a source is a modeling decision, not a
   simplification, and it must be recorded as one.
2. **Mathematical analysis.** Address identifiability *before* running the optimiser. Which
   combinations does the data constrain? The chapter's discussion applies directly, and
   `ch8_route_b_identifiability.py` gives a numerical handle. A fit of a non-identifiable
   combination will converge and mean nothing.
3. **Numerical validation.** Fit, then interrogate: refit from multiple starting points, check the
   optimiser reached the same optimum, and examine residuals for structure. On synthetic data,
   check whether the true parameters are recovered and whether the intervals cover them.
4. **AI-collaborative audit.** Ask an AI system to critique the fitting strategy — loss function,
   weighting, treatment of initial conditions, and the ascertainment assumption. The chapter shows
   ascertainment being handled badly in published work; whether the AI raises it unprompted is
   itself worth recording.
5. **Disclosure.** Log every interaction, including suggested reparameterisations that were rejected.
6. **Final memo.** The fit, the uncertainty, the identifiability analysis, and what the fit does not
   establish.

## Deliverable

A memo of roughly 3,000–4,500 words plus fitting code. The audience is a modeler who will reuse the
parameter estimates: they need to know not only the values but what the values are conditional on.

Required: a figure showing fit against data with uncertainty; a table of fitted parameters with
intervals and what each is conditional on; and an explicit statement of what the fit cannot support.

## Assessment

Weighting is the instructor's. What follows is what to look for, not what each is worth; courses
differ in how much they weigh fitting technique against honesty about what the fit does not
establish, and these materials do not legislate that.

| criterion | what a strong submission shows |
|---|---|
| Preprocessing | choices recorded and their effect on the fit measured, not assumed negligible |
| Identifiability | addressed before fitting; non-identifiable combinations named |
| Fit quality and method | loss defended; multi-start convergence demonstrated |
| Uncertainty quantification | intervals with a stated method; on synthetic data, coverage reported |
| Disclosure completeness | rejected suggestions recorded |
| Honest limitations | states what the fit does not establish |

Weigh the identifiability discussion heavily. A fit presented without one is unpersuasive however
well it matches the data, because matching the data is the easy part and the chapter says so.

## Notes for instructors

**Scaling.** Requiring the synthetic route for part of the class gives a group whose uncertainty
quantification can be assessed against known truth, which makes grading the real-data projects much
easier by comparison. Mixing the two and comparing in a final session is the strongest version of
this project.

**Common failure modes.** Fitting before thinking about identifiability. Single-start optimisation
reported as if it found the global optimum. Ascertainment ignored, so $\mathcal{R}_0$ absorbs the reporting
rate. Reporting intervals from the optimiser's covariance without stating the assumptions that makes.
Treating a low residual as model validation.

**Team option.** Two students can fit independently to the same data with different loss functions
and reconcile, which surfaces the sensitivity to that choice more convincingly than one student
varying it. If teams are used, AI disclosure is per person and the memo names who fitted what.
Teams are offered, not required.
